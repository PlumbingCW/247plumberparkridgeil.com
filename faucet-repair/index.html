Six moments when a faucet turns into a plumber call

Faucet problems tend to show up at predictable moments. Find yours below, then read on for what to do.

A drip that never stops

A steady drip points to a worn washer, seat, O-ring or cartridge. It is the most common faucet repair, and the drip runs day and night.

Wet cabinet floor

Dampness under the sink means a supply line, an angle stop or a drain connection is weeping. A dry paper towel held to each joint shows which one.

Handle spins, water runs

A fixture that keeps flowing after you close it needs the water shut off upstream before anything else.

Spray or burst line

A braided supply line that fails can put out a lot of water fast. Treat it as an emergency.

Stiff, gritty handle

Mineral buildup or a worn cartridge makes handles hard to turn. Forcing one can snap the stem.

Cold-snap spigot

An outdoor hose bib that froze may crack inside the wall and leak only when you turn it on in spring.

Urgent: act now

Valve will not shut off or a supply line burst: act in this order

Treat uncontrolled water as an emergency, whether it comes from a cracked braided line, a snapped angle stop or a valve that spins and does nothing. Work from the nearest shutoff outward so you stop the flow with the least disruption. Our 24/7 line takes these calls at night and on weekends.

If the little valve under the sink will not turn, do not force it with pliers. A twisted stem can break and release more water. Go straight to the main valve instead.

  1. Turn the angle stop under the sink clockwise to close it
  2. If water still flows, close the main valve where the water line enters the house
  3. After the main is closed, open the lowest faucet in the house to drain the line
  4. Put a bucket and towels under the leak and move belongings out of the way
  5. Stay away from outlets and power strips near standing water
  6. Photograph the damage for your insurance claim before you clean up

Three valves between the street and your faucet

Water passes through three kinds of shutoff before it reaches a spout. The first is the angle stop, the small oval or round handle directly under a sink. Next is the main shutoff, usually near the water meter in a basement or utility room. The last is the valve at the street, which the city controls.

Old multi-turn valves wear out inside and can spin freely without closing, while newer quarter-turn ball valves are easier to trust in an emergency. Find your main valve before you need it, label it, and turn it closed and open once a year so it does not seize. A valve that moves stiffly today is the one that will not close during a burst.

Broken or burst supply valves and supply lines

A supply line is the braided or rigid hose that connects the angle stop to the faucet. The braided kind has a rubber core that ages, and the nut at either end can corrode. Failures often start at the coupling, and a line that looks fine can give way without warning.

An angle stop that drips at the stem, weeps at the nut or will not close is a separate problem. Replacing one means closing the main valve first, which is why a stuck main is the more serious case. If the main will not close either, tell us on the call so we can plan around it, and if a pipe behind the wall has also failed, see burst pipe repair.

We replace the stop and the line together, since a new stop on an old line leaves the weak link in place. Quarter-turn stops are easier to use the next time something goes wrong.

Showers, tubs and drains that share a visit

A shower valve leak usually lives behind the wall. Water that keeps coming from the showerhead after you shut it off means the cartridge or seats inside the valve are worn, while a drip at the head itself is often a washer or buildup at the arm. A wet wall or a stain on the ceiling below is a different matter and needs an access panel opened.

Tub faucet repair follows the same logic. A spout that drips usually has a worn cartridge or seat, and a spout that sprays from the shower riser as the tub fills has a diverter that is not sealing. Older tubs can need a matching cartridge that is hard to find, which sometimes tips the choice toward a new valve.

Shower and tub drains clog with hair and soap film long before the pipe itself is the problem. A strainer and a small hair tool clear the top few inches. When water backs up from the tub into a sink or toilet, the blockage is further down, and drain cleaning with a cable reaches it.

Ordinary faucet repair on a bathroom or kitchen sink follows the table above: identify the type, replace the worn cartridge or seals, and clear the aerator. A leak under a utility sink, from the supply or the trap, is fixed on the same visit.

Repair the faucet or replace it

Repair when

  • The body is sound and only a cartridge, washer or seal is worn.
  • Parts for your model are still sold.
  • The finish is in good shape.
  • The leak is at the supply line or stop, not the faucet.

Replace when

  • The body is pitted, cracked or leaking from the casting.
  • You have repaired the same faucet more than once.
  • Parts are discontinued or hard to match.
  • You want a different style, height or a pull-down sprayer.

Outdoor spigots and winter shutoff in Zone 5b

USDA hardiness maps place Park Ridge in zone 5b, where hard freezes are normal, so treat every outdoor spigot as a pipe that can crack. The University of Illinois Extension advises removing and draining all hoses from the spigots, because a hose left attached can hold water that freezes and pushes ice back toward the house.

A frost-free sillcock has a long stem that puts the valve seat inside the heated part of the wall, so the water stops where it cannot freeze. It also has to be installed with a slight downward tilt to drain. The same Extension article cautions that even frost-proof spigots can freeze, depending on how cold it gets and how well the wall is insulated.

Extension also suggests giving each spigot's supply its own shutoff with a bleeder valve, so you can close it for winter and drain the line. If a spigot cracked over the winter, it may leak only when you open it in spring, and water can spray inside the wall. Close the indoor shutoff and call us for same-day help when available.

Park Ridge note: who shuts off what, and who pays for which pipe

Park Ridge residents who need the water shut at the street after hours call the Police Department non-emergency line, and during business hours the Public Works Service Center takes requests (city FAQ). Closing the street valve does not stop the water bill, but it can stop a flood when your own main will not close. Know which valve you have before the night you need it.

The city maintains water meters, while the homeowner repairs the water service pipe between the buffalo box and the premises (Municipal Code 11-1-6 as quoted on the city FAQ). A leak between the street and your main is yours to fix, and water service line repair covers it.

The city's WaterSmart portal also sends leak alerts, which is handy for a slow faucet or valve leak, and leak detection can trace one that stays hidden. Bills arrive every other month, so a small leak can run a long time before the paper bill catches it.

Licensing and code for faucet work

Swapping a faucet is simple work, but the Illinois Plumbing License Law, 225 ILCS 320, still sets who may do plumbing for pay. According to the Illinois Department of Public Health, you can check any plumber's license on its online lookup before the visit.
