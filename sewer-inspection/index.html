Repair it, line it or leave it alone: the question a camera answers

Every sewer decision starts with the same unknown: what is inside the pipe. Without a look, a plumber can only guess whether a repeat clog is hair and grease, a root, or a crushed section. A camera replaces guessing with a picture.

That picture decides how much money you spend. It can show a clean, sound line that needs nothing, a single defect that needs a spot repair, or damage long enough to justify lining or replacing. See sewer line repair once the video points that way.

What the camera shows and what it cannot

What you see on screen

  • Cracks, broken sections and offset joints.
  • Roots entering at a joint or crack.
  • Sags or bellies where water sits.
  • Scale, grease and debris that narrow the pipe.
  • Where the pipe turns, joins another line or meets the street.

What it cannot tell you

  • Whether the city main in the street is sound.
  • Why a defect formed. The video shows condition, not cause.
  • Hidden leaks at fittings if the camera cannot pass them.
  • Anything past a blockage until the line is cleared.
  • Your insurance coverage. That is a policy question.

You see the video, and we mark the defects and explain what each one means.

Locating the line and the problem spot

The camera head carries a small transmitter. A hand receiver at the surface picks up its signal and shows the depth and position of the pipe, so we can mark a crack on your lawn or driveway.

That mark matters if a dig is needed. It tells you how far the defect is from the house, whether it falls under a tree or slab, and whether it sits on your lateral or out in the public street. We note it on the recording.

Getting a recording you can keep

Ask for the video file, not just a verbal report. A recording lets a second contractor give a bid, lets a buyer negotiate with the seller, and lets you compare the line after a repair.

Ask that the recording show the full run, with a footage counter or a note of distance. A video that stops at the first bend answers less than one that reaches the street. If the pipe needs cleaning first, ask us about hydro jetting as a separate step.

Reading the video

How to read the findings

You do not need to be a plumber. These are the shots that matter and what each usually means.

Clear and smooth

The pipe is intact, so a past clog was likely a plain blockage.

Roots in a joint

A root mat means the joint is open. Cutting roots helps for a while, but sealing the gap lasts longer.

Standing water in a dip

A belly. Waste settles there and the line may clog again.

Cracks or missing pipe

A broken section may need spot repair, lining or replacement.

Rough, flaking walls

Corrosion or scale. If the pipe is cast iron, it is a sign the interior is wearing.

Offset ledge

A step between pipe ends. It catches paper and can get worse over time.

Before lining: why the camera comes first

Lining puts a new resin pipe inside the old one. A municipal lateral lining program says its liner cannot be used on collapsed or back-pitched pipe and requires a recorded camera pass to confirm the pipe qualifies.

So the inspection is the gate. If it shows a clean, straight, intact pipe, lining can be an option. If it shows a collapse, a repair has to come first. Read the trade-offs on the sewer line repair page before you decide.

Park Ridge note: a cleanout makes every inspection easier

The Park Ridge city FAQ recommends a sewer cleanout for inspection access and, quoting Municipal Code 11-1-6, makes the homeowner responsible for the lateral up to and including the connection to the main. A cleanout is a capped pipe that gives direct entry to the line.

One county public works page describes a cleanout as often sitting a few feet from the house or near the front property line, and suggests a camera check every three to five years. That is that county's advice, not a Park Ridge rule.

If you cannot find a cleanout, a plumber can scope from a pulled toilet, but it takes longer. Adding a cleanout may need a permit, since Article 15 of the Municipal Code covers plumbing alterations. Permit rules vary by job, so we confirm with the Park Ridge Building Department. After a backup, sewer line cleaning and a scope together give the clearest picture.

Homeowner insurance usually does not pay for an inspection, since it is maintenance. If it finds a sudden covered loss, the recording supports your claim. Check whether your policy has a water backup endorsement before you buy or after you move in.

Standards for camera inspection

A camera inspection is observation, and what it finds feeds into repairs governed by the Illinois Plumbing Code, 77 Ill. Adm. Code 890. According to the city FAQ, the homeowner repairs the sewer lateral up to and including the connection to the sewer main, so the footage tells you which side owns the fix.
