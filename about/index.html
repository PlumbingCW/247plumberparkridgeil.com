Who we are and what we do

We built this service around one idea: when water is moving where it should not, you need a person on the phone, not a form. That is why the whole site runs on a single line, answered 24/7, including nights, weekends and holidays.

Our work covers the jobs Park Ridge homes and businesses call about most. That means drain and sewer problems, water heaters, leaks and burst pipes, sump pumps, toilets and faucets, and planned jobs such as repiping and basement waterproofing. The full list is on our plumbing services page.

Every visit comes from a licensed, insured plumber. You hear a clear price before work starts, and you can say no after hearing it.

Licensing and permits in Illinois

According to the Illinois Department of Public Health, plumbers, apprentices and plumbing contractors are licensed under the Illinois Plumbing License Law. The department publishes an online lookup, so you can check any plumber before you let them start work. We encourage you to.

Plumbing in Park Ridge also runs through the city. Article 15 of the municipal code requires a permit for plumbing installations or alterations, so larger jobs such as a water heater swap or a repipe involve the Building Department. Permit rules vary by job, so we confirm them before work begins.

What to expect when the plumber arrives

The plumber introduces themselves, confirms the problem with you and looks at it before touching anything. Then you get the price and a plain explanation of the options, including the cheaper one when one exists. Nothing starts until you agree.

When the work is finished, the plumber tests it, cleans up the work area and tells you what to watch for over the next few days.

If a problem needs a specialist, such as a heating contractor for a boiler issue or a waterproofing contractor for a foundation crack, we say so instead of guessing. Honest limits save you money and keep our plumbers on the jobs they do well.

Why Park Ridge plumbing has its own quirks

Park Ridge is a city of about 39,656 people on roughly 7 square miles, and most of its homes are owner-occupied single-family houses. Many sit on older sewer connections that tie into a combination sewer, which carries storm water and household waste in the same pipes.

That matters when it rains hard. The county's hazard mitigation plan records nearly 500 basements flooded on July 23, 2011, so a floor drain that gurgles in a storm deserves attention. We bring that history to calls about sump pumps, sewer cleaning and backups.
