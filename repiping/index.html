Repiping in Park Ridge, IL

Repiping in Park Ridge, IL means replacing the water supply pipes inside a house, usually because galvanized steel, polybutylene, or aging copper has started to fail. The new run is typically PEX or copper.

Older house, older pipes: what the year your home was built suggests

Pipe materials follow construction eras. Houses built through the middle of the last century often have galvanized steel supply lines. Later homes may have copper, and some have polybutylene, a flexible gray plastic pipe associated with fitting failures. Newer work is usually PEX or copper.

Age is only a hint, because remodels mix materials. A 1950s home might have copper in the kitchen and galvanized in the basement. The Park Ridge city page on lead service lines says homes built before 1986 are more likely to have lead service lines, which is a reason to look at the pipe where water enters, not just inside the walls.

Warning signs

Signs the supply pipes are wearing out

Brown or rusty water

Orange or brown water when a tap first opens, especially after a quiet stretch, can mean corroding steel.

Falling pressure

Mineral and rust buildup narrows old pipe, so showers weaken and two fixtures cannot run together.

Pinhole leaks

Tiny sprays or green crusting on copper, or repeated weeping at joints, show metal is thinning. A leak detection visit can confirm where.

Frequent repairs

Fixing one leak and finding another a few feet away is a pattern, not bad luck.

Stains on ceilings

Hidden drips leave rings on drywall long before a pipe fails outright.

Metallic taste

Water that tastes of metal after sitting overnight can point to the pipe, not the supply.

Galvanized pipe replacement

Galvanized pipe is steel coated with zinc, and the coating wears away over decades. Rust forms inside, flow shrinks, and the pipe can leak at threaded joints. The pipe can look sound outside while being nearly closed inside.

A plumber can cut a short piece out to see the inner wall. If it is heavily scaled, patching one leak tends to move the next failure to the weakest joint nearby. At that point a staged or whole-house repipe is usually the more honest recommendation. Rust-colored water that remains after the pipe is replaced is a different question, and water softener and filtration covers filtering.

PEX or copper for the new run

PEX tubing

  • Flexible, so it snakes through walls with fewer cuts and fittings.
  • Handles cold better than rigid pipe, though no pipe is freeze-proof.
  • Usually less disruptive to install.
  • Needs approval for use, which we confirm with the Building Department.

Copper

  • Long track record and familiar to inspectors.
  • More joints and soldering, so more time and heat in the walls.
  • Can still pinhole in some water conditions.
  • Often preferred where pipes stay exposed, such as a basement ceiling.

Staged repipe or whole house at once

Staged, floor by floor

  • Spreads the work and the disruption over several visits.
  • Lets you live in the house between phases.
  • Old and new pipe share the system for a while.
  • Works well when a basement run is the worst part.

Whole house in one project

  • One round of wall access and one round of patching.
  • No mixed metals left behind.
  • Most disruptive for a few days.
  • Best when leaks are showing up in several rooms.

Wall access, patching, and how disruptive it gets

Most repipes run new lines through basements, crawlspaces, attics, and closets, then open small sections of drywall where a pipe has to drop into a wall. A plumber can often reuse chases that already exist. Cabinets under sinks and tub access panels are common entry points.

You will likely have no water for part of each working day, so plan showers and laundry around it. Dust, holes, and open ceilings come with the job, and drywall patching is often a separate trade. A whole-house repipe usually takes days, not hours, and a staged job stretches across several visits.

Permit and inspection under Article 15

The Park Ridge city FAQ says Article 15 of the Municipal Code requires a permit for plumbing installations or alterations, and written permission from the Building Department before any material change. A certificate of inspection and approval is required before the new system is used. New fixtures or lines added during the same project fall under plumbing installation. Permit rules vary by job, so we confirm with the Park Ridge Building Department.

Park Ridge note: a city of owner-occupied houses

The city community profile counts 14,384 housing units in Park Ridge, with 82.8 percent owner occupied and 77 percent single-family detached. Most repipes here are therefore decisions about a home someone intends to keep, which favors replacing a failing run once rather than patching it for years.

Park Ridge buys its water from Chicago, and the 2020 report lists no lead or copper results above the action level at 30 sample sites. The city report does not publish a hardness figure, so ask for the annual water quality report and test your tap if you are weighing copper. The report also advises flushing the tap after water sits for several hours.

Permits and code for a repipe

A repipe is an alteration, so it falls under the Illinois Plumbing Code, 77 Ill. Adm. Code 890. According to the City of Park Ridge resident FAQ, Article 15 of the Municipal Code requires a permit for all plumbing installations or alterations. The city FAQ also calls for a certificate of inspection and approval before a new plumbing system is used, so plan for an inspection before the walls are closed.
