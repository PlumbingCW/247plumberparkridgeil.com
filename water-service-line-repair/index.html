Your house, your pipe: what age and layout tell you about the line

The service line is a single pipe, but its story depends on the house. Older homes may still have the original line, and the material can be lead, galvanized steel, copper, or plastic. Newer additions and remodels often add a second tie-in or a relocated entry, which creates joints that can fail.

The line usually enters through a basement wall or floor, and the main shutoff sits just inside. Where the pipe passes through foundation masonry, settling and frost movement can stress it. Knowing where yours enters tells a plumber where to start looking and which stretch to dig.

What you notice

Signs the line under your yard is failing

Pressure drops

A leak or a clogged old line cuts flow at every tap, often worst when two fixtures run.

Soggy ground

A wet strip, a sinking spot, or a puddle in dry weather points to water leaving the line.

Meter runs

The meter keeps moving with the house main closed, so the loss is on the line before it. A leak detection visit can pinpoint the spot.

Brown or cloudy water

Rust or sediment stirred up by a failing pipe, or by repairs elsewhere on the line.

Sound at the entry

A hiss or hum where the line enters the basement can mean a small leak under pressure.

No water at all

A fully broken line or a stuck valve. Check the main before assuming the whole street is out.

Emergency water service line repair: when water is running out of the ground

A broken line can wash out soil, flood a basement window well, or leave a house with no water. We take these calls around the clock, including overnight and weekends, and dispatch can tell you what to shut off on the phone.

  1. Close the main valve inside the house, turning it clockwise until it stops.
  2. Look for water at the meter and the buffalo box so you can tell dispatch which side it is on.
  3. Keep children and pets off soft or sinking ground over the line.
  4. Move stored items away from basement walls where the line enters.
  5. If you cannot stop the flow and it is after hours, the City asks residents to call the Police Department non-emergency line for a street shutoff.
  6. Do not dig on your own. Buried gas and electric lines may sit nearby, and 811 can mark them.

Lead service lines: what Park Ridge says and what to check

The Illinois Lead Service Line Replacement and Notification Act took effect January 1, 2022. Park Ridge's own page says the City has begun its service line material inventory, covering both the City and private sides, that residents whose lines were identified as lead have been notified, and that a public dashboard is online. It also says homes built before 1986 are more likely to have lead lines.

The City's 2020 water quality report listed no lead results above the action level at 30 sample sites and noted that lead comes mainly from service lines and home plumbing, not from the water source. The report advises flushing the tap after water sits for several hours. We cannot tell you whether your line is lead from the outside.

Check your own pipe where it enters the house, since lead is soft, dull gray, and scratches silver. Then ask Public Works about the inventory entry for your address. Pipes inside the house that are galvanized or old copper are a separate question, covered under repiping.

Low water pressure at every tap

Low pressure that reaches the whole house suggests a service line or main valve problem, not one clogged aerator. Try a hose bib and a basement tap. If both are weak, partially closed valves, a leak on the line, or an old narrowed pipe could be the cause.

A pressure gauge on a hose bib reads the line pressure, and a comparison between inside and outside readings narrows the stretch. Weak pressure that appears on the street side of the buffalo box is a Public Works question. If only one fixture is weak, faucet repair is the likelier fix.

The house end: valves, meter connection, and new water lines

Where the service line meets the house, three things matter: the main shutoff, the pipe through the wall, and the connection to the meter, which the City maintains. A main valve that will not close all the way is a common finding on older homes and is worth replacing while the line is exposed.

If you are adding a bathroom, a laundry, or an outdoor tap, the new water line ties into the house side of this system. Planning it with the line repair avoids opening the same wall twice, and our plumbing installation page covers that side.

Trenchless or open trench: honest trade-offs

Trenchless (pit-to-pit)

  • Small access pits at each end, so less lawn, driveway, and landscaping damage.
  • Works best on a straight, accessible run.
  • May not suit lines with sharp bends, collapses, or crowded utilities.
  • Method availability should be confirmed with the Building Department.

Open trench

  • Exposes the whole pipe, so every fitting and the valve can be inspected.
  • Fits short runs, shallow lines, or ones with several repairs.
  • More digging, restoration, and temporary loss of lawn or paving.
  • Simple to inspect and document for the permit.

Permits and inspection for water line work

Park Ridge's city FAQ says Article 15 of the Municipal Code requires a permit for plumbing installations or alterations, and a building permit is also needed for work on water supply and piping. A certificate of inspection and approval comes before a new plumbing system is used. Permit rules vary by job, so we confirm with the Park Ridge Building Department.

What drives the cost of a water service line job

Two repairs on the same street can look nothing alike once the ground is open. For your real number, call our 24/7 line.

Length and depthA short, shallow run is a smaller job than a deep line that crosses a driveway or a mature tree root zone.
Repair or replaceA single failed spot differs from a full replacement of an old line, especially if the material is lead or galvanized. A fast split in a nearby pipe falls under burst pipe repair.
MethodTrenchless needs entry pits and special equipment. Open trench needs more digging and restoration.
Surface to restoreSod, a concrete walk, and an asphalt driveway are very different to put back.
Permits and inspectionPermit review and inspection scheduling add steps that affect how soon the line returns to service.

A homeowner policy may cover sudden water damage inside the house but often excludes the underground pipe itself, and some insurers sell a separate service line endorsement. Check your policy and ask the insurer before work starts.

Park Ridge note: Chicago water, City meters, and the buffalo box

Park Ridge buys all of its drinking water from Chicago, which draws from Lake Michigan, and the City maintains the water meters. That means a pressure complaint can start on the City's side of the buffalo box, so it is worth a call to Public Works before anyone opens your yard. That call can save a dig.

The city's lead page says it must complete its service line inventory by 2027 and replace all lead lines by 2044. If your line is flagged, repairing a break might become the moment to replace the whole run. Ask Public Works before spending on a short repair of a pipe the City may retire.

Responsibility and permits for the water service line

Under Municipal Code 11-1-6, as quoted on the city FAQ, the homeowner repairs the water service pipe between the buffalo box and the premises. According to the City of Park Ridge resident FAQ, Article 15 of the Municipal Code requires a permit for all plumbing installations or alterations. Work follows the Illinois Plumbing Code, 77 Ill. Adm. Code 890.
